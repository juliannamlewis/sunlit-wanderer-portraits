## This should look like them

A senior session is a strange assignment, because two people usually want two
different things from it. The senior wants images that feel like them — a little
more personality, a little more edge, something they'd actually post. The parent
wants something timeless for announcements, for the relatives, and for the wall
at graduation.

Both are reasonable. Both are achievable in one session. They just need a few
minutes of planning first so we come away with each.

## Bring what matters to them

The difference between a senior portrait and a nice photograph of a teenager is
usually an object. A sport, a uniform, an instrument, ROTC or 4-H gear, medals,
a career-tech project, artwork, a gaming setup, a horse, the truck they saved up
for two years to buy.

You don't have to. But a year from now, the frames that hold up are almost
always the ones with something of theirs in them.

## How the posing actually works

I show a few ideas. They tell me what feels right and what feels absurd. I talk
them through it from behind the camera, and I show them the back of the camera
as we go so they can see for themselves that this is going fine.

I never physically move anyone. Every adjustment is spoken.

Most seniors are stiff for about four minutes and then forget to be. That's
normal and I'm patient about it.

## Outfits

Roughly one look for a Short session, one or two for a Standard, two to four for
an Extended — which also gives us room for a second nearby setting when it makes
sense.

Wear things that reflect who they are. Accessories are welcome: a hat, a scarf,
sunglasses, a jersey. Clean shoes, no giant logos unless the logo is the point.

I have a pop-up changing tent I can bring for multiple looks. Just tell me ahead
of time.

## Which session fits

**Short** — one look, one location, the frames you actually need for
announcements.

**Standard** — a second look and room for the personality shots, not just the
portrait.

**Extended** — multiple locations in one evening. A typical run is about 40
minutes at the farm and creek at Carriage Hill, 30 in the Oregon District, and
20 at the Art Institute. They're close enough together to make that realistic.
Two to four outfits, three completely different backdrops, one senior year
documented properly.
